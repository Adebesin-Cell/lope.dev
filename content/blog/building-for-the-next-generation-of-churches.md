### What it means, how we take care of it, and where we are.

![Building for the next generation of churches. The BethelFlow mark above a winding, dashed route with a few wrong turns, passing a small house, a hall, a church, and finally a cluster of churches.](/images/blog/building-for-the-next-generation-of-churches/cover.svg){loading="eager" fetchpriority="high"}

I've been on a bit of a tour recently. Lagos, Ibadan, Ogun State. New cities, new roads, and me trying to map every direction as I went.

I got lost a lot, Lagos especially 🤭. Wrong turn, wrong bus stop, a street that looked right until it very much wasn't. There were moments I just stood somewhere and felt stranded.

But the part that surprised me was that I wasn't bothered. I was calm, and honestly, I was having fun, even though it wasn't the best of situations. A few years ago I would have been panicking. This time I just figured it out one turn at a time, and in between all of it, I kept thinking about one thing.

What's next for [BethelFlow](https://www.bethelflow.com/)?

## A few Sundays

I attended a few services along the way. A big Sunday, the kind where the car park is its own ministry, and a small one, where everyone knows everyone and the person who welcomes you is also the person who'll read the announcements.

And then CCI, which I really enjoyed. As a first timer, the welcome felt very similar to BethelFlow's own onboarding, which was a funny thing to sit through from the other side 😅. After I'd settled into my seat, they handed first timers a set of cards to go through, some notes, a reminder and a little guide to settling in. Someone had clearly thought about what it feels like to be new in a room that big.

At every service I caught myself watching the welcome desk, watching how first timers got picked up, and noticing the moment someone tries to fill a form on their phone and the network in the auditorium just isn't there. And I kept seeing things we could do to help, small things mostly, the kind a church would never think to ask software for, because nobody's really asked them what's hard.

I'd love to chat with them. Really. Sit down with the welcome team, the children's church volunteers, the person who counts the offering at the end, and just ask.

## The wave we're not seeing yet

So it got me thinking. The next generation of churches is starting, and it's already here, we're just not aware of it. Or perhaps we're not yet seeing how big a wave it's going to be.

It doesn't look like one thing. It's the home cell in someone's living room, the church plant meeting in a school hall with folding chairs, the diaspora church that started as a prayer meeting and now runs more than one service, and the large bodies of churches we already have today, with branches in cities their founders never visited.

And it's bigger than I thought. There are about 4.7 million congregations in the world right now, and the [Center for the Study of Global Christianity](https://www.worldchristiandatabase.org/static/downloads/Status-of-Global-Christianity-2026.2b54be19fc0c.pdf) expects 6.1 million by 2075. I did the arithmetic on their table, and that works out to roughly 240 new congregations a day, even after counting the ones that close. So somewhere today, a couple of hundred churches had their very first meeting, probably in a living room or a hall.

A lot of that growth is happening in places like the ones I was walking around in. Africa alone has around 780 million Christians and is projected to pass 1.8 billion by 2075. And wherever people move, churches start with them, which is why London, between 2005 and 2012, was seeing [two new churches open every week](https://www.eauk.org/church/one-people-commission/stories/black-and-ethnic-christians-lead-london-church-growth.cfm), most of them Pentecostal and Black-majority.

We've not even seen the peak yet.

## One thing I have to be honest about

I really wanted to quote the UK "Quiet Revival" numbers here. You probably saw them last year, young people going back to church, with attendance among 18 to 24 year olds jumping from 4% to 16%. It went everywhere.

It got [withdrawn in March](https://www.thetablet.co.uk/news/bible-society-pulls-flawed-quiet-revival-report/). The survey behind it had fraudulent respondents in it, and the Bible Society pulled the report. The more careful UK data shows [no big youth revival](https://natcen.ac.uk/publications/there-religious-revival-britain), at least not in the national figures.

To be honest, I find that more interesting than the headline. So many of us wanted that story that a bad poll went viral. The real picture is less dramatic and a lot more useful, because the growth is uneven and happening in pockets, in evangelical and Pentecostal churches, in diaspora churches, in young churches most surveys don't even reach. Allison Norton, one of the researchers at Hartford, [put it better than I can](https://www.covidreligionresearch.org/study-rise-congregation-attendance/): *"What we're seeing is not a revival — it's a recalibration."*

Churches aren't disappearing. They're changing shape.

## What does it mean?

Think about the big Sunday and the small one I went to. They're both church, but they're not the same problem at all.

Most churches look a lot more like the small one. In the US, the median congregation is about [70 people](https://www.covidreligionresearch.org/study-rise-congregation-attendance/), and 90% have 250 or fewer, even though most churchgoers end up in the big ones. And most church software, if we're being honest, is built for the big ones, the ones with a paid admin, an IT person and a budget line for tools.

The small ones are run by people who already have a lot on their plate. A quarter of US senior pastors [seriously considered quitting](https://www.barna.com/trends/pastors-quitting-ministry-barna-data/) in the past year, mostly because of the stress of the job, and in the UK, two thirds of evangelical churches say they don't have [enough people for children's work](https://www.eauk.org/assets/files/downloads/Changing-Church-2025-FINAL.pdf). It's the small Sunday again, where the person who welcomes you is also the person reading the announcements.

People belong differently now too. The average churchgoer attends about [1.6 times a month](https://www.barna.com/research/young-adults-lead-resurgence-in-church-attendance/), so someone can be deeply part of a church and only be in the room twice a month. Some watch online, some come to cell on Wednesday and skip Sunday.

So "who was here on Sunday" is kinda the wrong question. The right one is closer to "who are our people, and is anyone looking out for them?"

That's what building for the next generation means to me, software that knows a church is people and not a headcount.

## How do we take care of that?

A few things I keep coming back to.

**Start where the church actually starts.** For a lot of churches, that's a home cell, not a Sunday service, and if a tool only understands the Sunday, it misses where most of the pastoral care happens.

**Make the first visit count.** The CCI cards stuck with me because they said *we noticed you*. A first timer shouldn't have to fill a long form on bad network to be remembered, and when they come back a second time, someone should know.

**Keep the old ways working.** Most US churches still [pass the plate](https://research.lifeway.com/2025/07/08/most-churches-still-pass-the-offering-plate/), even though most also take gifts online. Digital got added next to the old way, it didn't replace it, and I think the next generation of churches will be hybrid in everything, not just their services.

**Take the weight off the few people carrying it.** Whether it's the volunteer at the children's church door or the pastor doing admin at 11pm, if software gives them more to do, it has failed, no matter how many features it has.

**Grow with them.** The living room becomes a hall, the hall becomes a building, and one day one church becomes a few branches in different cities. The tool should be the same tool the whole way.

## Where are we?

We at BethelFlow are building for the next generation of churches, and for the ones already here.

A lot of it is in real use today, across the UK, Italy, Nigeria and Canada. A first timer gets logged at the welcome desk and a welcome message goes out on its own. Children get checked in and only go home with someone who's allowed to pick them up. Giving works whether it comes online or in an envelope, and follow-ups go out without anyone having to remember to send them.

And some of it we're still building. A guest taps a pass, and the church recognises them when they come back, then sends a welcome back on its own. A church with branches gets to see itself as one tree, not a pile of separate accounts. And the piece this whole post has been circling, we're already building cells, so home cells and small groups become a real part of the church, with their own people, their own meetings and their own care, not a note someone keeps in a spreadsheet, because that's where the next generation of churches is starting.

There's a lot more I want to build, and I'd rather build it by listening than by guessing. Which brings me back to the chats I want to have.

## Getting lost, calmly

I think the reason I was calm in Lagos is that I stopped expecting to know the whole map before I started. I knew roughly where I was going, and I trusted I'd find the next turn when I got to it.

That's where a lot of churches are right now. A pastor starting a cell in their living room doesn't know it'll be a church of hundreds in ten years, and a church with one branch doesn't know it'll have twelve. They're mapping new directions as they go, and a lot of the time, it probably feels like being lost.

We want to be something that helps them find the next turn, and then the one after that. Not the whole map, just the next turn.

So if you run a church, serve in one, or just started one in your living room, come tell me what's hard. I'd really love to hear it. I'll be around ❤️
