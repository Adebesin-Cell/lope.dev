You're made of magic.

This is my favorite phrase. What does it mean? Magic is something that's lively. Small or large, it can always change the way everything is.

My name is Tolulope Adebesin, and I know I'm made of magic. 😇

## The bet

I think the very first moment was thinking about what I wanted to do as a high school graduate. I asked about different options, but none of them felt fair to me. I wanted to be a tailor, or teach at a local school (I recall saying "why are high school graduates paid so little and expected to do more work?"). I even thought about being a fuel seller 😄.

I wanted to keep busy. I didn't want to learn the usual Excel and Word. I felt those were skills I already had.

Then I recalled that I had learnt some HTML in junior high. I picked it back up and tried a few PDFs. I also joined multiple Slack channels and asked if they had a discount for a student who would pay them back. The fees were incredibly expensive and I had no money, but I knew: bet on me, and see.

I took the weird route. I saw a hub in my area and messaged them for a volunteer internship. I had nothing in my portfolio 🤣, so I told them I'd be back in 3 months with something, at least.

I did go back, and I spent a year with them. They are called Zealarax Technologies, and I'm grateful for every single one of them on that team. ❤️

## Zealarax

Somewhere in between, I lost my dad, and it felt like everything was going to end. With more support, I found my way to keep learning frontend at Zealarax.

I loved my time at Zealarax. It was an escape from thinking too deeply about my loss. I was there 8 to 8, learning and building. There were moments of self-doubt and late nights, but they were all fun 😇.

Mr Font, Mr Habeeb and Mr Shina were incredible mentors. They made sure I could take part in the projects they had going, whether as a QA person, working on small features or even owning a feature. We worked on the University of Abuja website redesign and the Federal Polytechnic Ilaro design. Looking at those sites now, they seem to have drifted far from the original designs we scoped 😇. That is where I was taught attention to detail and the quality of the work.

## FUTA

I started at the Federal University of Technology, Akure, in October 2021, to study Computer Engineering. I wrote JAMB and scored 331. I still wonder how I got that score 😂. I wrote the post-UTME too and did quite well, though at the time my score felt low to me.

Year one was me trying to adjust. I have a very shy personality, and I still do. It takes a lot out of me to approach someone. My shyness is a strange one, though. I can be extremely confident and shy at the same time. And I live a lot in my head.

So my friends mostly found me. In 100 level, my friend Mayowa found me to teach Sets to the whole CPE class, and somehow I tagged along with him. He introduced me to his friend group. They became my friends, the people I graduated with, and they are some of the most amazing guys ever. Everyone else I met the usual way: your friends bring in their friends 😅.

I started strong, with a 4.8 GPA in my first semester and about 4.6 in the second. Then my grades crashed in 200 level. Work had become difficult. I was sponsoring my own schooling, and as a first child, I had to support at home in some ways 😅. School took the hit, though I tried to balance it out.

At some point my laptop battery died, and for a few months the laptop only worked plugged in. I saved my pocket money and bought a new battery. Then it was alive again. I spent that time working on a few projects and met several people I got the chance to work with briefly.

Then in 2022, ASUU went on strike, and I went back to Zealarax. It wasn't the full house anymore, but it still gave me room to work. I spent most of those months gathering more resources, and I sat for my first interviews. I failed them. Partly the network, partly that I was still very early. I don't remember the company names. One was in Budapest. The other was looking for a junior Angular developer. When I asked him what I did wrong, he said I was persistent. He could tell I was new to interviews.

It was humbling. I think I was about to give up on tech and look for whatever came next.

## IQ.wiki

Around that time, I made a WhatsApp redesign page and [posted it on Twitter](https://x.com/I_am_Lope/status/1563144532639682561) (probably my first ever post on Twitter, to be honest). The engagement brought a lot of people, and somehow it got me to the door of Cesar Rodriguez, the CTO of IQ.wiki. He asked if I was available for a role and what my university schedule was like. He said that was fine, because most of the team were early university graduates or still in school, so there was no need to worry.

I joined as a junior frontend engineer and worked on IQ.wiki. My first task was to replace a blog link. That was around 4 years ago, and it has been amazing work. I went from junior frontend engineer to product engineer, and took on more responsibility across the BrainDAO ecosystem.

Over time, titles stopped meaning much. We worked on whatever the company was focused on at the moment, and the scope moved with it.

That took me from IQ.wiki to AI agents and, most recently, [TuPredices](https://www.tupredices.com/), a prediction market that is on a waitlist for now.

Across all of it, I review most of the PRs 😅, and I love it. I read them by hand to nitpick, often to correct patterns we had already agreed on, and to share things I've read that the team could benefit from.

The IQ.wiki rewrite, and the harsh reviews that came with it, is its own story. I wrote it up in [Predictable codebase](/blog/predictable-codebase).

## Open source

I joined the Chakraverse a year and some months ago. Most of that story is already written in [A year, a verse and an ecosystem](/blog/a-year-a-verse-and-an-ecosystem), so I won't tell it twice here.

Zag changed how I build UI. I learnt what I'd call composition UI: breaking a UI into states that are predictable and readable. An error shows an error, loading shows loading, and there aren't many gymnastics to render the UI. It makes the UI very performant and very easy to reason about.

Gymnastics is Sage's favorite word, from what I can find 😅. He pushes back on the weird patterns I sometimes come up with.

Building UI in states has been a game changer. Every series of events becomes a state you can name: on hover something happens, you click a button and something fires, and the reaction has to work. It's a good way to think, because a machine has a fixed return. In third year, a course in school reminded me of state machines, though I had already heard about them from a colleague by then. They're somewhat different, but the base idea is the same.

States just make it easy to build. Everything is a state.

What I'm up to now is trying to build better maintainer qualities. I want our open source projects to be in great shape, and I want us to build better connections with the community so that we are reliable and people use what we make.

It's not the easiest of roles. Andrew Nesbitt's [Now Hiring: Senior Open Source Maintainer](https://nesbitt.io/2026/08/28/now-hiring-senior-open-source-maintainer.html) is satire, but it's close. You try to wear many hats. It's a part-time role, but I hold it with genuine accountability, so you'd think it was my full-time job.

This site now runs on the libraries I help maintain, which I covered in [Practice what you maintain](/blog/practice-what-you-maintain). Panda v2 is out now, on a new engine written in Rust. I told the story of how it started in [New engine, new origin](/blog/new-engine-new-origin), and in October I gave a talk at [SailsConf](https://sailsconf.com/) on rebuilding it.

"Painless is an accessibility feature" is a line I read a while back. I don't remember the blog, but it talked about how much friction you can remove by being precise about what the user wants. If I have to fill a form with validation, don't wait until I've filled the whole form to tell me something is wrong. Don't reload everything every time I come back to a page. Make it painless.

Pain can be a feature too, though. There are moments where a user should feel some friction, and the UI shouldn't let them get away with everything.

## BethelFlow

It started after a contract project Drey and I worked on together. When it ended, I started quizzing him on what was next haha. After a while, he brought an idea, and we started something together. We started BethelFlow.

We built it with a small team. I led the frontend, Drey worked on mobile, and a few more people joined to help maintain the backend. It was a very small team, and we started building. Combining it with school was one thing 😅. I took on more than I could carry, but I kept going.

I brought the practices I learnt at IQ.wiki, the ones from [Predictable codebase](/blog/predictable-codebase), to the team. We had rough days, but we kept building. Our first church was [RCCG Amazing Grace Chapel](https://rccgamazinggracechapel.org/about/index.php), and they have been with us for more than a year.

### Why church software

We started BethelFlow so churches could focus on the other parts of their work. Someone has to do the job. You see so many manual record forms in churches, and records go missing. The people who run the church pay the price, in pain. Metrics and proper data, even simple things like who came to church today and who didn't, are usually managed by one or more departments. They often get missed, and over time tracking becomes difficult. A system in place makes that much better.

It's quite boring work, but it does more than a lot of things. It's great to see church software on the rise.

We had so many ideas about becoming the operating system for churches, and we are still working towards that. We keep hearing feedback from people about how we can be better, and we put it together so we can cater for very different church types, countries and even small groups. We want to be at the bedrock of every church, now and in the future, worldwide.

### What building it taught me

This is the second year of building, and we launched a year ago. It's one of the best and most challenging things I have done so far. I realised that what I do shapes the organisation, so I have to do more, be better and learn as quickly as I can. There was less time to feel sorry for myself. I make mistakes, and I learn from them.

When you co-found a software project, you automatically divide the roles, CEO and CTO 😂. Drey became CEO and I became CTO. I do my best to lead and to keep the team moving in the direction of the organisation.

Being CTO is actually very difficult, though it's not that much different from what I did before. It's more of a headspace haha. You have to hire people who have the same drive for the product as you, make sure that drive stays there, and make sure people are learning properly.

Recently, I've been trying to be a better lead and to get involved across the whole project, even marketing, which I had never done before. I bring what I've learnt from open source to the team, and we've made our security stronger. I've also been contributing to the backend to help our backend developer, so we can move faster and answer what users ask for 😄.

Speed is poison, sometimes 😄. In the AI age you can move fast, but you have to do what is right. Some things you look at and say "uh uh, we are not going in that direction", or they feel too complex for a very small need. What is right eventually turns out best, and the best way to find it is to listen to the people who use your product. That's where trust is built, and over time it compounds.

A few BethelFlow stories already have their own posts: [My Pastor Once Said](/blog/my-pastor-once-said), [The button that did nothing](/blog/the-button-that-did-nothing) and [The quietest kind of bug](/blog/quietest-bug).

I have a lot more to say about building BethelFlow. It's my baby, and I want to see it reach the light.
